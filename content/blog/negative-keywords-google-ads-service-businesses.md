A Toronto contractor can pay for a click from someone looking for a job, a free tutorial, or a service outside its coverage area. A negative keyword tells Google Ads when **not** to show an ad for a search. Used carefully, it helps protect budget for enquiries the business can actually serve.

The mistake is to copy a huge “universal” negative keyword list into every account. A word that is irrelevant to one campaign may describe a valuable customer in another. The better process starts with your own search terms and your definition of a qualified lead.

## What negative keywords do

A negative keyword excludes searches containing or matching a specified term, depending on its match type and where the exclusion is applied. Google lets advertisers add them at the ad group, campaign, list, and account levels. An account-level negative applies broadly to eligible search and shopping inventory, so it needs the most caution. See Google's guides to [negative keywords](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/2453972?hl=en) and [account-level exclusions](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/11396330?hl=en).

Negative keywords do not repair a weak offer, a slow landing page, or poor follow-up. They solve a narrower problem: ads appearing for searches you do not want to buy.

## Start with the search terms report

Open the [search terms report](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/13738234?hl=en) for a useful date range. Add the keyword and match-type columns, then review the actual searches that triggered ads. For each term, ask:

1. Does it describe a service we sell?
2. Is the searcher in an area we serve?
3. Is the searcher likely to be a buyer, or are they researching a job, course or DIY project?
4. Did this term produce a qualified enquiry, appointment or customer?
5. Would excluding it also block a valuable variant?

Sort by spend and group similar terms. A single expensive query deserves attention, but one click is not enough to declare an entire theme worthless. Compare the search terms with your CRM outcomes where possible; our [guide to lowering Google Ads cost per lead](/blog/lower-cost-per-lead-google-ads) explains how search intent and conversion tracking work together.

Google notes that the search terms report can reveal irrelevant queries and help you add negative keywords. The report's “Match type” column describes how a search term related to the keyword that triggered the ad; it is not always the same as the match type you assigned to that keyword. [Google explains the distinction](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/2472708?hl=en).

## Choose the narrowest useful exclusion

Consider a residential HVAC company that does not recruit technicians through its ads:

| Search theme | Possible action | Check before excluding |
| --- | --- | --- |
| “HVAC technician jobs” | Exclude the job-seeking phrase or theme | Would any hiring campaign need it? |
| “how to fix furnace yourself” | Exclude a clear DIY phrase | Could an emergency repair query contain the same word? |
| “commercial refrigeration repair” | Exclude if that service is not offered | Does another business unit serve commercial clients? |
| “furnace repair Toronto” | Keep if the business serves Toronto | Does the landing page match the service? |

These are **examples**, not a ready-made list. Service areas, job types and customer language differ. Review the query and its context before adding an exclusion.

Google Ads supports negative broad, phrase and exact match in Search campaigns. The choice changes how much traffic a term can block. Use a narrow exact or phrase exclusion when a broad term could suppress legitimate searches. Google warns that negative keywords can overlap with regular keywords and stop an ad from showing; its [setup instructions](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/7102995?hl=en-en) explain the notation and placement.

## Pick the right level

- **Ad group:** Use when a term is wrong for one service group but relevant elsewhere. A “repair” group might exclude “installation” while an installation group keeps it.
- **Campaign:** Use when the term is wrong for every ad group in a campaign, such as a service the campaign never offers.
- **Shared list:** Use for exclusions that apply to several campaigns, then review every campaign before attaching the list. [Google's negative keyword list guide](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/7449003?hl=en-en) explains how changes to a shared list apply to the campaigns using it.
- **Account:** Reserve for terms that are genuinely unwanted across the relevant account inventory. A new service or campaign may change that answer later.

Keep ownership clear. If an agency or employee adds exclusions, record what was added, where, why, and when it should be reviewed.

## A weekly review routine

For a small service business, a short, consistent review is more useful than an occasional mass cleanup:

1. Review high-spend search terms and recent qualified-lead outcomes.
2. Group irrelevant searches by intent: jobs, DIY, unsupported services, wrong geography or another clear pattern.
3. Add the narrowest exclusion that addresses the pattern.
4. Check overlap with your valuable keywords and campaigns.
5. Record the change and watch impressions, qualified leads and cost per booked appointment afterward.

Do not judge success only by a lower cost per click or fewer impressions. If qualified enquiries fall, revisit the exclusion. The goal is to spend less on **irrelevant demand** while preserving searches that bring real work.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should every service business exclude “free”?

No. “Free” may describe a job seeker or a DIY search, but it can also appear in a valuable query about a free estimate or audit. Look at the full search term and your actual offer before excluding it.

### Are negative keywords the same as positive keyword match types?

No. Negative match types determine which searches are excluded. Google documents differences in how negative and positive matching work; do not assume a negative will block every close variant of a positive keyword. Review the [negative keyword guidance](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/2453972?hl=en).

### Can exclusions fix poor lead quality by themselves?

Only when irrelevant search intent is the cause. Lead quality also depends on geography, ad copy, the landing page, qualification and response speed. An audit should examine the full path from search to booked job.
