A Toronto business owner opens a Google Ads report and sees a promising number: leads cost $40 each. The phones are ringing, forms are arriving, and the campaign appears to be improving. Then the calendar tells a different story. Few enquiries become appointments, and fewer still become paying customers.

The problem is that cost per lead measures the price of an enquiry, not the value of that enquiry. A form from someone outside your service area counts as a lead. So does a call asking for a service you do not offer. If those enquiries are cheap enough, they can make a campaign look successful while your team struggles to book work.

The answer is not automatically to spend more or make every form longer. First, find out where the gap between enquiry and booking begins.

## Why cost per lead can hide poor booking performance

A higher cost per lead is not always better. The comparison is incomplete until you know which enquiries your business can serve, which people your team reaches, and which conversations become bookings. For a business with a longer sales cycle, you may also need to follow appointments through to accepted jobs.

## Check the searches behind your leads

A keyword describes the searches you hope to reach. The search term is what a person actually typed before seeing your ad. Those two can differ.

Imagine a Toronto renovation company that wants full basement projects. Its ads may also attract searches about basement materials, small repairs, DIY instructions, jobs, or projects outside its minimum scope. Some searchers will still complete a form. They are real people, but they are not necessarily prospective customers for this offer.

Start with the [Google Ads search terms report](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/13738234?hl=en). Review terms alongside the leads they produced, rather than judging them only by clicks or form submissions. Group them into three buckets:

- Good fit: the requested service, location, and project type match what you sell.
- Uncertain: the search could fit, but the enquiry needs more context.
- Wrong fit: the person wants something you do not provide.

Add negative keywords for clearly irrelevant themes, but check the wording before excluding an entire category. A research-oriented search might still lead to a valuable project later. Google also does not expose every individual query in the report, so use it as evidence, not a complete record of demand. [Search terms insights](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/11386930?hl=en) can add broader categories to the picture.

## Review location targeting and service fit

A business can serve Toronto without serving every address, every job type, or every appointment time equally well. An emergency service may have a tight dispatch area. A contractor may travel farther for a large renovation than for a minor repair. A clinic may accept patients from across the GTA but have limited appointments at one location.

Google Ads location settings deserve a close look here. The default targeting option can include people who are in or regularly in a location and people who have shown interest in it. Google says location detection is an estimate and is not perfectly accurate. Review the [advanced location options](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/2453995?hl=en-en), user-location reports, and your actual lead addresses before changing the setting.

Then compare geography with business outcomes. A lead from outside your usual area is not automatically poor; it may be worthwhile for a high-value project. Equally, a lead from inside Toronto is not automatically profitable if travel time, appointment availability, or job size makes it a poor fit. Let completed work and intake notes define the boundaries.

## Align your ads and landing page

A low cost per lead can begin with an attractive promise that is too broad. “Affordable renovations,” “fast appointments,” or “free consultation” may bring enquiries, but each phrase creates an expectation. If the landing page or sales team later reveals a minimum project size, limited availability, or a different service, the lead may disappear before booking.

Read your ad and landing page as a customer would. Do they agree on the service, location, price expectation, and next step? If the ad offers an emergency repair, can someone actually reach the emergency team? If it promotes a consultation, does the page explain what that consultation covers?

Be clear enough that the wrong-fit visitor can make a decision before contacting you. This may reduce raw lead volume. If it raises the share of enquiries that book, it can still improve the campaign. The change should be measured against bookings, not celebrated or rejected on form volume alone.

## Confirm what counts as a conversion

Before diagnosing targeting, confirm what Google Ads calls a conversion. Is it a completed form? A click on a phone number? A call of any duration? A visit to a thank-you page? If two tags fire for the same submission, one enquiry may appear twice.

Also check which actions the campaign uses to optimize bids. Google distinguishes [primary conversion actions](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/11461796?hl=en), which can be used for bidding, from secondary actions that are generally observed in reporting. A campaign optimizing toward every easy form submission has no way to know, from that signal alone, which submissions became useful conversations.

Do not switch goals based on a small sample of appointments. First make sure the existing events fire correctly and only once. Then compare your Google Ads counts with the forms, calls, and appointments recorded by your team. ## Separate lead quality from follow-up

Not every unbooked lead is an advertising failure. A prospect may have requested exactly the right service and still gone elsewhere because nobody answered the call, a callback came too late, or the booking process was unclear.

Separate lead fit from lead handling. For a useful sample of recent enquiries, record whether the team reached the person, how long the first response took, why an appointment was not booked, and whether a later follow-up succeeded. Keep the reason categories simple enough that staff will use them consistently.

For example, “outside service area” points toward targeting or page messaging. “Wanted a service we do not offer” points toward search terms or ad copy. “No answer after three attempts” needs a different response from “we called two days later.” Lumping them together as “bad leads” sends the campaign team in the wrong direction.

## Audit a sample of recent enquiries

You do not need a complex dashboard to start. Take a manageable sample—perhaps the last 30 to 50 Google Ads enquiries—and give each one a single outcome: qualified and booked, qualified but not booked, wrong service, wrong area, unreachable, duplicate, or unknown.

Next, compare those outcomes with the campaign, search term where available, location, ad promise, and landing page. Look for a repeated pattern before changing anything. If one service produces many wrong-fit calls, review its terms and copy. If several campaigns produce suitable leads that do not book, inspect the intake process first.

Choose one meaningful change, record when you made it, and watch both enquiry volume and booked appointments. Changing keywords, location settings, forms, and bidding goals at the same time makes it difficult to learn which fix worked.

## Measure qualified leads and booked appointments

Keep cost per lead in the report; it is useful for spotting changes in enquiry volume. Put it beside three numbers that explain quality:

1. Qualification rate: qualified enquiries divided by all enquiries.
2. Booking rate: booked appointments divided by all enquiries.
3. Cost per booked appointment: ad spend divided by booked appointments.

If your CRM reliably records accepted jobs, add cost per customer or cost per won job. Compare service lines separately where their values and buying cycles differ. An urgent repair call and a planned renovation estimate should not be judged by the same booking timetable.

When your records are dependable, later outcomes can be sent back to Google Ads through [enhanced conversions for leads](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/10029210?hl=en). That is a measurement project: define the stages, check consent and data handling, test imports, and confirm the numbers before relying on them for bidding. The immediate win is simpler—stop treating every enquiry as equally valuable.

A Toronto Google Ads campaign is working when it brings people your business can help and gives them a practical path to book. If your cost per lead looks good but your calendar is thin, follow a sample of enquiries from search to appointment. The leak may be in the query, geography, promise, tracking, or follow-up. Once you know which one it is, you can fix that part of the journey instead of buying more of the same leads.
